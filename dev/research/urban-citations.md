# Urbanisation: the threshold problem, properly sourced

## The single strongest piece of evidence
Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson, "Reversal of Fortune", QJE 117:4 (2002), Appendix 3.
**Mexico, urbanisation in 1500, same region same year, by method alone:**

| Estimate | Value | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Chandler (1987) | 6.5% | Chandler's own |
| Bairoch (1988) | 7.0% | 5,000 |
| Eggimann (1999) | 12.3% | 20,000 |
| AJR base (Eggimann -> Bairoch-equivalent) | 14.8% | 5,000-equiv |
| Davis-Zipf adjustment (Eggimann x2) | 24.6% | 5,000-equiv |

Peru 1500: Chandler 2.5% / Eggimann 5.8% / Bairoch 12.0% / AJR 10.5%. A fivefold
disagreement, probably reflecting genuine uncertainty about whether Inca administrative
centres (Huanuco Pampa, Tomebamba) were cities or seasonal state installations.

AJR's conversion regression: constant 6.6, coefficient 0.67. World mean urbanisation 1500 on
their base data: 7.3% (s.d. 5.0).

**AJR refuse to give sub-Saharan Africa a figure at all**: "Because there are no detailed
urbanization data for sub-Saharan Africa, we leave this region out of the regression analysis
when we use urbanization data." The leading quantitative economic-history dataset declines.

## Other threshold evidence
- EC/GHSL (via Our World in Data) found the world **85% urban in 2015 against the UN's 55%**.
  A 30-point gap from definition alone.
- UN WUP **2025 revision switched to DEGURBA**, under which the world has been ~80% urban since
  at least 1950, against the UN's own earlier announcement that it crossed 50% in 2007. Live
  methodological break for anyone plotting a UN series.
- National thresholds: Sweden 200; Ethiopia and Liberia 2,000; Mexico 2,500; India 5,000 plus
  75% non-agricultural employment; Botswana and Zambia 5,000 plus non-agricultural majority;
  Nigeria 20,000; Japan 50,000 plus employment criteria.
- Across 78 countries using a threshold definition c. 2015: median 2,500, mean 4,362
  (Jedwab, Johnson & Koyama, IIEP-WP-2020-9).
- US Census 2020 criterion changed to "at least 2,000 housing units or a population of at least
  5,000", up from 2,500 population. The housing-unit pathway is new.

## The agricultural-town argument, properly framed
Paul Wheatley, "The Significance of Traditional Yoruba Urbanism", CSSH 12:4 (1970), 393-423.
Tests Yoruba settlements against Wirth's definition and finds they satisfy EVERY criterion
except social heterogeneity. The careful framing is not "these weren't really towns" but
"these were towns on every axis but occupational differentiation".

- Kano 1824, Hugh Clapperton: ~40,000 residents, of which **three quarters of the walled area
  was laid out in fields and gardens**.
- Goddard, "Town-Farm Relationships in Yorubaland", Africa 35:1 (1965): of 204 people in Oyo's
  Abu lineage, 85 were permanent urban dwellers and 119 lived in the country while retaining
  town allegiance. His emphasis: the tie is ceremonial and religious, not economic.
- **1952 Nigerian census, Yoruba, same population same year**: 53.5% in communities of 5,000+;
  44.8% at 10,000+; 36.6% at 25,000+; 22.1% at 100,000+. The cleanest single illustration.
  (Probably originates with Bascom, AJS 60:5, 1955 - reached via secondary source, unverified.)

## Two findings that bear on the data
- **Teotihuacan/Basin of Mexico**: Cowgill (Annu Rev Anthropol 1997): between 100 BCE and 200 CE
  "most of the Basin of Mexico population was relocated in the city". A regional urbanisation
  rate near 50% in the first centuries AD would be extraordinary by any world standard.
  Basin of Mexico 1519 ~1M+, Tenochtitlan ~212,500 = ~20% in the single primate city before
  counting Texcoco, Tlatelolco or Cholula. (Derived arithmetic, not stated by any source.)
- **Nigeria's 59.77% (2020) is measured against a 20,000 threshold**, one of the world's
  highest. Its real share on a 5,000 line would be SUBSTANTIALLY HIGHER than 60%, the opposite
  of the usual intuition. Combined with contested census counts, the least trustworthy number.

## Australia, confirmed and striking
ABS: **57.5% urban in 1911** (US was ~46% in 1910), 90.3% in 2021. World Bank: 81.59% in 1960,
already above most of Europe. Melbourne 23,000 (1851) -> 280,000 (1880) -> 490,000 (1890).
Sub-Saharan Africa went 14.34% (1960) -> 42.17% (2020): roughly the move Europe took a century
to make, in sixty years, without industrialising.
Papua New Guinea has been flat since 1980 (13.06% -> 14.17%), so an aggregated "Oceania"
number blending an 87.6% Australia with a 15.4% PNG is close to meaningless.

## Pre-contact Oceania: nothing reaches 5,000
Nan Madol "almost certainly did not exceed 1,000" and was not residential. Maori pa: 5,000+
sites but "few people lived long-term in a single pa", seasonally abandoned 4-6 months a year.
Hawaii settlement was dispersed by ahupua'a, not nucleated. On the Dark Emu dispute (Pascoe vs
Sutton & Walshe, Keen, McGlade, Grieve-Williams): even Pascoe's highest claim is a town of
1,000, well under any threshold. Nobody argues anything reached city scale.

## UN WUP 2025 switched methodology (Nov 18, 2025) -- the biggest caveat of all
WUP 2025 is dual-track: it still publishes national definitions, but now ALSO publishes a
harmonised geospatial series (DEGURBA, on the EU JRC Global Human Settlement Layer).
Uniform thresholds on 1 km2 grids: cities >=1,500/km2 AND >=50,000 in cluster; towns and
semi-dense >=300/km2 AND >=5,000; rural below that.

**Sub-Saharan Africa, same region same years, two rules:**
| Year | national definition | DEGURBA cities+towns |
|---|---|---|
| 1950 | 11.3% | **42.7%** |
| 2000 | 32.7% | 60.8% |
| 2025 | 45.1% | 70.3% |

The "Africa was barely urban in 1950" story is substantially an artefact of national
definitions. The UN effectively concedes this: "in sub-Saharan Africa, many semi-dense/town
populations are not fully counted as 'urban' in national terms" (WUP 2025 Methodology, p.5).

The World Bank has already migrated to WUP 2025, so "World Bank vs UN" is no longer a live
discrepancy. The real discrepancy is WUP 2018 vs WUP 2025: Nigeria was revised **+8.2pp** at
2018 (50% -> 58.16%), Kenya +4.4pp.

## Bairoch's Africa figures, and the convergence check
Bairoch (1985/1988) at a 5,000 threshold: Africa 5% (1900), 12% (1950), 28% (1980).
Exact trio is literature-attributed, not directly verified in a primary text.
**Convergence check: Bairoch's 28% for 1980 against World Bank/WUP 2025's 27.42% for Africa
in 1980.** That is a good agreement and supports using a consistent 5,000 rule for this
dataset rather than the UN's national definitions.
Jedwab & Vollrath (2015) confirm Bairoch's threshold is 5,000, and that across 81 countries
using an explicit threshold the median is 2,500 but the mean is 4,362, "not far from the 5000
threshold used by Bairoch".

## Africapolis: the same continent under one rule
OECD/SWAC. Two criteria applied identically everywhere: continuous built-up area with <200m
between buildings, and >=10,000 agglomerated inhabitants. Found **7,617 urban agglomerations
across 50 African countries** against 210 in UN WUP at the time. Levels are higher than
officially reported in **25 of the 50 countries covered**.
Sharpest single case: **Onitsha, Nigeria. Official population 1.1 million; Africapolis
agglomeration 8.5 million**, over an area 80 times larger than the administrative city.
Counter-case: **Egypt defines only administrative capitals as cities and their number has
barely changed since 1960, so its official urbanisation has been stagnant around 43% for half
a century.** Verifiable in UN data: 43% in 1990, 43% in 2018, 42.9% in 2025.

## Nigeria's series is effectively unanchored
Definition is "towns with 20,000 inhabitants or more" (among the world's highest thresholds).
Sources per UN WUP 2018: "Census of 1953; Estimates for 1960, 1970, 1980, 1990, 2000 and
2010." **One census, from 1953**, plus modelled estimates, plus a cancelled 2011 census, plus
an 8.2pp revision between WUP vintages. Any visualisation that renders Nigeria at the same
visual confidence as South Africa is overstating what is known. Direct argument for the
Phase 8 confidence layer.

## Urbanisation without industrialisation
Gollin, Jedwab & Vollrath, J Econ Growth 21(1) 2016. Across 116 countries in 2010 the expected
relationship between urbanisation and industrialisation is ABSENT, driven by resource
exporters. Angola: 15% urban before oil in the 1960s, 60% in 2010, while oil is over 50% of
GDP and employs fewer than 10,000 nationals. Botswana: diamonds 36% of GDP, 13,000 employees.
Mining averages 1% of total urban employment across the sample. "Consumption cities" versus
"production cities". Jedwab & Vollrath (2015): across 1500-2010, urbanisation is 25-30
percentage points higher in 2010 than in 1500 AT EVERY LEVEL OF INCOME PER CAPITA.
