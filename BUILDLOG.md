# Build Log

A historical population-dynamics visualization engine, built in three sittings between July 29 and August 3, 2026: 20 commits, 18 GitHub issues, one self-contained index.html with no server and no dependencies. The current dataset renders Christian and Muslim population share from AD 33 to 2026; the engine is designed so other population types (planned: more cultures and movements over history) are swappable datasets, not rewrites. The historical estimates are unaudited, and that caveat is repeated at the bottom where the shortcomings live.

**Jul 29, 4:20 PM. A sandbox with rules.** The ai_projects monorepo came first: a home for small toy projects and one-shot ideas, with a PROJECTS.md index and status vocabulary (idea, active, playable, parked, promoted). The heatmap was its first resident.

**Jul 29, 4:36 PM. A playable v1 the same afternoon.** The first commit of the project is already the whole idea working: an interactive world heatmap of Christian population share, AD 33 to 2026, hybrid model (hand-curated regional anchor data blended with a logistic growth and diffusion simulation seeded at Jerusalem), timeline scrubbing, parameter sliders. Getting a v1 running is the cheap part now. The three sittings, eighteen issues, and one caught data error that follow are where the judgment lives, and they are the reason the thing is worth opening.

**Jul 31, evening. The honesty wave.** Interior America looked empty because population-weighted rendering hides sparse-but-devout regions, so a per-capita display mode joined the default (#4). The HUD learned to stay pinned under zoom (#5). The Islam layer arrived (#1) with a co-presence rule that took real design care: purple only when both faiths genuinely share a cell, with a ratio band so a 10 percent / 60 percent split reads as the majority it is, not as parity. The issue title says the quiet part: "honest disparity in blend mode" (#8).

**Aug 1. The data challenge.** A spot check caught the model claiming Dearborn was around 3 percent Muslim. Wrong, and caught by eye: that figure describes the county, not the city. The fix became its own issue (#2): city-scale overlay regions carrying modern diaspora demographics honestly at true footprints (Dearborn, Greater Paris, the Ruhr, Greater London), with shares from Pew and the UK 2021 census, and a documented note that France bans religious censuses so French figures are softer estimates. The dataset overall remains unaudited; this pass proved the correction loop works when a number fails the eyeball test.

**Aug 3, afternoon. The camera earns its keep.** In-app pan and zoom to 60x with the coastline switching from 110m to 50m detail past 3x (#6 phase A), a full-window toggle, and the engine's best trick: the heat field is not a pixel grid but gaussian weight points evaluated analytically over whatever the viewport shows, so detail sharpens as you zoom at constant cost. City-scale points narrower than a cell get sigma clamped up and amplitude scaled down by the area ratio, mass-conserving anti-aliasing, so Dearborn honestly dilutes to a few percent of its 2-degree cell at world view and resolves to a city-footprint spot at 50x.

**Aug 3, evening, part one. The world gets crowded and mortal.** Post-1975 Muslim diaspora hotspots across Europe and the US (#7), old-world city densification so no stretch of history goes 95 years without a new city appearing (#9), and city lifecycle events (#10): conquests telegraph in the attacker's color and burst into the new name, failed sieges flash and revert (Vienna twice, Malta), dead cities fade for good (Carthage, with Tunis rising nearby). All animations are pure functions of the playhead year, so scrubbing backward replays history in reverse for free. A running population tally HUD with square-root-scaled bars keeps the first millennium legible (#11).

**Aug 3, evening, part two. Three visualizations sharing one engine.** The biblical narrative layer was scoped as an epic (#12) and shipped as five phases in one evening (#13 through #18): a mode framework treating World demographics, Apostolic Age, and Old Testament as separate visualizations that share the map, camera, and timeline; a journey and annotation rendering engine; Paul's four journeys leg by leg with the Malta shipwreck; Pentecost igniting the fifteen nations of Acts 2; and a church history overlay drawn with the same engine as Paul's journeys, which is the point of the visual rhyme. Provenance is rendered, not footnoted: solid lines are scripture-attested, fine dashes are historically attested, hollow markers are tradition, and a checkbox hides the tradition tier entirely, visibly thinning the map.

**Since then.** In occasional use, fully pushed.

**Sep 5, 2026. The sandbox dissolves.** The heatmap turned out to be the only resident `ai_projects` ever had, so the repo became the project: files flattened to the root, the sandbox index and its status vocabulary retired, an MIT license added, and the whole thing renamed `history-heatmap` ahead of going public. Commit authorship was rewritten at the same time to correct a machine that had been signed in under the wrong identity. The eighteen issues stay where they always were, which is why every issue number cited above still resolves.

**Sep 15, 2026. The datasets become swappable, and the map admits what it doesn't know.** The plan cited below as "still a plan" got built. Christianity and Islam had been hardcoded as a pair through roughly fifty call sites, so the first move was a registry refactor landed with output byte-identical across fifteen probes covering seven years, three zoomed frontiers and every control state — the property that made the rest safe to attempt. Then Buddhism, and urbanisation as a different *kind* of dataset: faiths contest each other for colour, a measure cannot, because a population can be 90% Christian and 90% urban at once.

Three findings changed the design. The purple co-presence blend cannot survive a third faith: the blue-to-red blend sweeps the entire hue space a third hue would need, and every candidate measured within a few ΔE of some contested blend under simulated deuteranopia. Contest had to leave the hue channel, so three or more faiths stripe instead, and the blend stays for the pair where it is still true. Saffron turned out to be unavailable for Buddhism at any price — to a deuteranope it is the Islam red — so the hue was chosen by scoring the whole circle at matched lightness, and Buddhism is teal. And the diffusion passes could not be run concurrently: `buildPressure` fills one module-level array refreshed every 24 sim-years while a frame covers 120, so two passes sharing frames would have applied each other's collapse eras in silent, non-deterministic stretches. A serial queue instead.

Adding Buddhism forced Southeast Asia apart. One region had been averaging a roughly 80% Buddhist mainland with a roughly 85% Muslim archipelago and burying a roughly 90% Christian Philippines inside both, asserting a blend nobody lives in. Splitting it surfaced that the old population array ran about 1.6 times high before 1850 — a consistent ratio across eleven anchors that converged to correct after 1900, which reads like a modern figure back-extrapolated on a smooth curve. Mongolia turned out to be in no region at all: 3.6 million people simply absent from the map.

The honesty work is the part worth keeping. A boot check confirmed that faith shares cannot sum above 1, and found 18 cells already over — Caucasus at 1.07 across nine anchors, because it holds Azerbaijan against a Christian share pinned at .88. It reports rather than corrects, because which way to move those numbers is a question about history. Confidence is now rendered instead of footnoted: fills wash toward grey in proportion to how much is actually known, with a separate flag for quantities that are not measurable even now. And the unmodelled slot greys the majorities the model does not carry, so India stops reading as Muslim-leaning when it is Hindu-majority.

Two features make the data legible rather than merely visible. A region inspector draws every dataset's whole curve on click, with dots for the anchor estimates and a line for the interpolation between them, because the map cannot show you where the curated data stops. And a compare view renders change rather than state: pin AD 600, scrub to 800, and the Islamic expansion and the Carolingian missions sit in one frame.

Three bugs are worth recording because they rhyme. The same temporal-dead-zone mistake shipped three times — a feature's state declared in its own section but read by the deep-link block, which runs at parse time and throws, taking the render loop with it. The fix was structural, not local: group the declarations, say why in a comment, and add probes that load with every deep-link parameter at once. Separately, the compare view's cached field was invalidated by callback, and a camera change that bypassed the callback left it sampling the wrong place — producing a map that showed Christianity *gaining* across the Levant in the century it was conquered. A cache that silently samples the wrong place renders a plausible picture that is entirely false, so it validates itself now.

**Known limits, stated plainly.** The dataset is the big one: the historical estimates are assembled from standard scholarship (Stark's early growth rates, dated conversions, Pew and World Christian Database figures) but have not been audited by the author, which is exactly why the project is filed as a toy despite the engine being defensible. The simulation is loosely tethered to history by design and will diverge if you crank the sliders. Dating in the Old Testament mode follows the traditional early chronology and is contested throughout. The zoomed render is over a 60fps budget and always was: `buildViewField` is 18.5ms of a 23.6ms frame at 8x, because a broad gaussian clips to most of the viewport there. The analytic field has no land mask, so a small region beside a huge one is diluted by its neighbour's population mass — Sri Lanka renders well below its true Buddhist share because India carries 68 million people against its 1.2. And Pakistan and Bangladesh are absent from the dataset entirely, roughly 430 million people in 2026, which is a larger hole than anything the region split fixed.
